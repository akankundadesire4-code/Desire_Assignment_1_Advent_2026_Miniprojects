Mini-Project 1: UBOS District Population Forecaster
====================================================
Plain-language notes for questions the assignment asks us to explain.

1. Why do statistics.variance and np.var give different numbers?
   - statistics.variance() always divides by (n - 1). This is called the
     "sample variance". It is used when your data is a SAMPLE from a bigger
     population and you want an unbiased estimate.
   - np.var() by default divides by n (the "population variance"), because
     NumPy assumes you already have the whole population.
   - The ddof argument (delta degrees of freedom) controls this. np.var(x)
     uses ddof=0 (divide by n). np.var(x, ddof=1) divides by (n - 1), which
     matches statistics.variance() exactly.
   - In this project we treat the 10 years of data as a sample, so ddof=1
     is the fair comparison to statistics.variance().

2. Which district is growing fastest in relative terms?
   - We answer this using CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate), not the raw
     year-on-year percentage changes, because CAGR smooths out yearly noise
     and gives one single "average yearly growth rate" for the whole period.
   - The district with the highest CAGR value is the fastest-growing in
     relative (percentage) terms, even if another district added more
     people in absolute (thousands) terms. See the notebook's printed CAGR
     table for the exact ranking with this specific data.

3. What does comparing forecast variance to actual variance tell us?
   - If the forecast variance is much smaller than the actual variance, our
     model is producing an overly smooth, "safe" forecast that may
     understate real year-to-year swings.
   - If the forecast variance is much larger, the model may be
     over-reacting to short-term noise in the training data.
   - Because our simple models (straight line, constant growth rate) are
     smooth by construction, we generally expect forecast variance to be
     lower than the actual historical variance. This is a limitation, not
     a sign of a "better" forecast.

4. Is the Fibonacci-ratio model defensible?
   - The Fibonacci-ratio model scales the last known value by the ratio of
     consecutive Fibonacci numbers (which converges towards the golden
     ratio, about 1.618, for large Fibonacci numbers, and is close to 1 for
     the smaller pairs).
   - It is not based on any real cause of population change (births,
     deaths, migration). It only "looks reasonable" because Fibonacci
     ratios happen to shrink and settle down, similar to how real growth
     rates often slow over time.
   - It could only be defended as a rough numerical curiosity for
     short-horizon forecasts, never as a real demographic model. Our test
     results (MAE/RMSE/MAPE) show whether it happens to fit this specific
     dataset better or worse than the linear and CAGR models.
